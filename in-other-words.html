In Other Words

3 Oct 2026

A crossword app that started with a puzzle I made for a friend. I began with how it should move and worked backwards to the screens. Built over a few days in October, and still moving.

For a friend

I made a crossword for a friend, filled with phrases, inside jokes and little references that probably wouldn’t make much sense to anyone else. I sent it over as a link, and while the idea was nice, the execution left quite a bit to be desired. It was cobbled together, the link expired, and solving it wasn’t particularly enjoyable.

I kept coming back to the idea, though. There’s something quite nice about making someone a puzzle out of things only they would recognise. So I decided to build it properly.

You can now make a crossword for someone using your own words and clues, then send it over as a surprise. A little puzzle made up of things only the two of you would understand.

Over coffee

I solve a crossword most mornings. It’s become part of my routine, and I quite like having something to work through over coffee.

I’ve also always been interested in the stats behind it. How long a puzzle takes, how often I need a hint, which clues catch me out and which ones I eventually give up on. Not because I’m particularly competitive about it, but because I like seeing the little patterns that emerge over time.

So I wanted to keep a proper record of it all. Every attempt is saved, finished or otherwise, with the grid exactly as you left it. You can go back through old puzzles, compare times, follow your streaks and see how your solving changes.

It’s a small thing, but I like being able to look back and see how I’ve been doing. A little archive of all those mornings spent solving.

Making it move

As a motion designer, I tend to think about how something moves before I think about how it looks. I started with the interactions and worked backwards to the screens, which felt like the right way round for this project.

A crossword has quite a particular rhythm. You’re moving between squares, switching direction, jumping between clues and occasionally changing your mind halfway through an answer. There are lots of small interactions, and I wanted each of them to feel considered.

One thing I spent quite a while on was the cursor. Originally, it travelled across the grid to whichever square you tapped. It worked, but never felt quite right. I eventually realised the movement was coming from the wrong place: it should grow out of the square you tapped, rather than the one you were leaving. So I changed it to do just that, leaning back in the direction it came from.

A small detail, but one that made the whole thing feel much better.
