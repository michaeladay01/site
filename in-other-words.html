In Other Words

3 Oct 2026

A crossword app, started from how it should move and worked backwards to the screens. I’m a motion designer, so that felt like the right way round. Built over a few days in October, and still moving.

Over coffee

I solve a crossword most mornings. The puzzles are great; the apps they come in are not. They feel dated, and they make simple things harder than they need to be. Finding the day’s crossword, picking up where you left off, looking back at how you’ve been doing — all of it is more work than it should be.

I wanted one that felt considered, in how it looked and in how it worked. A crossword has its own rhythm. You move through the grid, jump between clues, hold two or three answers in your head and watch everything fall into place at once. The app around that should feel as good as the solving does.

I also wanted a proper record of how I solve. Not a streak to protect, but a way to see whether I’m getting better. How long a puzzle takes. How often I need a hint, and which clues make me reach for one. Which ones I give up on. Small things that add up to something about how you think.

Most crossword apps either don’t keep this or bury it. In Other Words saves every attempt, finished or not, with the grid exactly as you left it. You can go back through your puzzles, follow your streaks, and watch your solving change.

That’s really all I wanted: a crossword app worth coming back to in the morning.

Between Friends

This part wasn’t in the plan. I made a crossword for my friend Kris — inside jokes, things we’d done together, references that wouldn’t mean much to anyone else — and sent it to him.

It worked, sort of. It was also pretty janky: cobbled together, the link expired, and solving it was nowhere near as nice as it should have been. But the idea was lovely. There’s something in making someone a puzzle out of things only the two of you know.

So I built it properly. You write a handful of words and clues about someone, and the app builds a crossword around them, filling the gaps with ordinary words to hold the grid together. As you write, it offers words in grey that would fit what you’ve already put down, so you can tap one to finish a corner instead of staring at an empty square. Even when none of them is right, it shows you which letters would sit well there — usually enough to think of your own.

Then you send it. They get it as a surprise, and when they finish it, an envelope opens with your message inside. A few shared memories turned into something someone can sit down and solve. More personal than your usual crossword.

Motion Design

Motion usually gets treated as the finishing touch — something you add once the screens are done, to make everything feel a bit more polished. I think it does more than that. It’s part of how an interface talks to you: what just happened, what’s happening now, whether it heard you.

Solving a crossword asks a lot of small questions you shouldn’t have to think about. Where did that letter come from? Which square am I in? Did my tap register? Can I keep typing, or is it busy? Motion answers all of them without a word on screen, and you stop having to work it out.

My favourite example is the cursor. It used to travel across the grid to whichever square you tapped. It worked, but it felt wrong, and it took me a while to see why: the movement belonged to the square you were leaving, not the one you’d chosen. So I made it grow out of the square you tapped instead, leaning back the way it came. You touch a square, and that square answers.
