An Internet We Remember

8 Nov 2026

I keep finding myself nostalgic for the internet of the mid-2000s — not the dial-up or the questionable downloads, but the feeling that it was a collection of places to explore, each with its own personality. So I rebuilt a very specific moment of it: 2005.

There’s something about the internet of the mid-2000s that I find myself increasingly nostalgic for.

Not necessarily the technology itself, or the dial-up connections and questionable downloads, but the experience of being online. It felt like a collection of places to explore, each with its own personality, and you had to find your way through them yourself.

I grew up with the internet around the time it was still finding its identity. Windows XP was my desktop, MSN was where I spoke to my friends, and YouTube was somewhere you went to watch a video someone had sent you. Music came from LimeWire (and occasionally a file that turned out to be absolutely nothing like the song you wanted), emails lived in Hotmail, and an afternoon online could involve jumping between eBay, Flash games, random websites and conversations with friends.

The internet felt like a place.

You had an identity there. A display name, a profile picture, a carefully chosen status message that was probably a song lyric aimed at someone in particular. You customised things, downloaded things, shared things and figured things out. Every website had its own look, sounds and little quirks. Nothing felt particularly standardised, and I think that was part of the charm.

Today, so much of what we do online happens within a handful of platforms. We open an app and are presented with an endless stream of content, with algorithms deciding what we see and what comes next. Even the platforms themselves have started to feel quite similar.

I think what I miss is the feeling of having somewhere to go, rather than just something to scroll through.

Somewhere to go

There were personal websites, blogs, forums and little online communities, all with their own identities. You could stumble across someone’s website, spend an hour clicking around and end up somewhere completely unexpected. People made things simply because they wanted to. There was a sense that anyone could have their own little corner of the internet.

I also think we used to spend more time making our online spaces our own. Your MSN display name, your MySpace page, your blog layout, even your desktop wallpaper. You could spend ages getting everything exactly how you wanted it. And then change it all again a few days later.

There was something fun about that. Your online identity wasn’t necessarily a personal brand or a carefully curated collection of content. Sometimes it was just a picture of your favourite band, a song lyric and a very important away message.

Rebuilding 2005

I wanted to revisit that feeling by building something that brought me back to a very specific moment in time: 2005.

I started with Windows XP and built outwards, bringing together the websites, applications and little rituals that I remembered from being online at the time. YouTube, eBay, MSN, Hotmail, LimeWire and a collection of games.

I wanted it to feel like sitting down at a computer and deciding what to do. Not opening a browser, checking three apps and somehow ending up watching a video about a stranger’s kitchen renovation.

Getting the details right was a big part of it. I didn’t want a collection of nostalgic references that all looked like they belonged to different years. Everything needed to feel like it existed at the same time. The desktop icons, window styles, browser, colours, typography and all the little details that make an operating system feel familiar.

But I also wanted things to actually work. You can open applications, move windows around, browse websites, compose an email, click into a YouTube video and even simulate downloading something through LimeWire.

I spent just as much time thinking about the interactions and sounds as I did the visuals. There are little things you might not consciously remember, but as soon as you experience them again, they feel familiar. The way windows open, the sounds applications make, the excitement of seeing a download progress bar move (although perhaps not quite as much excitement as we had back then).

Building it became less about recreating how the internet looked and more about recreating what it felt like to use it.

Old internet, new tools

There’s something quite funny about using today’s technology to rebuild an internet that existed before so much of today’s technology did. I had access to tools that would have absolutely blown my mind in 2005, all so I could spend my time recreating a slightly dodgy LimeWire download.

I wasn’t trying to make something more efficient or polished. Quite the opposite, actually. I wanted to keep some of the character and imperfections that made the original experience so memorable.

I don’t think I’m particularly nostalgic for slow connections, having to wait for a song to download or accidentally downloading the wrong version of a song 14 times. But I do miss the sense of discovery, the individuality of different websites and the feeling that the internet was somewhere you could actively explore.

Building this was a nice excuse to spend some time back there, and to revisit a version of the internet that feels quite different from the one we use today.

Now, if you’ll excuse me, I have some very important MSN conversations to get back to.
